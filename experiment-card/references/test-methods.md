# Test methods

Ordered roughly from cheapest to most expensive. Start at the top and move down only when a cheaper method couldn't produce evidence strong enough to change the decision.

| Method | What it is | Best for | Evidence it can give | Typical cost & time | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Desk research** | Find what's already known: studies, statistics, comparable schemes, people who've tried it. | Any lens, first. Often kills or reshapes an idea for free. | Up to 2 | Hours to days | Evidence from a different context; confirmation bias in what you search for |
| **Expert or practitioner calls** | Short conversations with people who've run something similar. | Feasibility, viability, hidden operational problems | 2 | Days | Experts' own biases; survivorship (you'll mostly reach schemes that still exist — seek out ones that closed) |
| **Problem interviews** | Open conversations with the intended audience about their current situation — *not* pitching the idea. | Desirability: is the problem real, how do they cope now, what have they tried? | 1 (for "whether"), strong for "why" | 1–2 weeks, 6–12 people | Leading questions; talking about the solution too early; recruiting only enthusiasts |
| **Existing-data analysis** | Use data you already have — bookings, enquiries, membership, web analytics. | Desirability and viability, if you already run something adjacent | 2–4 depending on the data | Days | The data may describe current users, not the people you want to reach |
| **Fake door / smoke test** | Show a real offer (a page, a poster, a button) for something that doesn't exist yet, and measure how many take the next step. Tell them honestly afterwards. | Desirability at small scale: will people take a first step? | 3 | 1–3 weeks | Never take payment for something that doesn't exist; be honest at the "door"; traffic must come from the real audience |
| **Clickable prototype test** | Real people try to complete tasks with a realistic prototype while thinking aloud. | Usability and comprehension; which step people drop out at | 3 for "can they", 1 for "would they" | 1–2 weeks, 5–10 sessions | Small samples show problems, not rates; testers try harder than real users |
| **Concierge** | Deliver the service by hand to a few real users, openly, with no automation. | Desirability and feasibility together: does it work end-to-end, would they use it again? | 4 | 2–6 weeks, 5–20 users | Doesn't test whether it scales; staff effort hides real costs — track them |
| **Wizard of Oz** | Users think they're using a finished service, but people behind the scenes do the work by hand. Tell them afterwards. | Desirability when the "real" feel matters | 4 | 2–6 weeks | Honesty and consent; don't let it run so long it becomes deception |
| **Pre-sale / pledge** | Ask for a real commitment — a deposit, a membership, a pledge — before building. Refund if it doesn't go ahead. | Viability: will people pay, at what price? | 4 | 2–4 weeks | Be explicit about refunds; early adopters pay more readily than the mainstream |
| **Small pilot** | Run a limited real version for a set period with clear stop rules. | Viability and impact over time; retention | 5 | Months | Expensive; hard to stop once started — set the kill threshold in writing first |
| **A/B or split test** | Show two versions to comparable groups and compare behaviour. | Choosing between versions of something already working | 3–5 | Weeks | Needs enough volume to separate signal from noise; don't use with tiny samples |

## Matching method to lens

- **Desirability** → problem interviews to understand, then fake door or concierge to measure behaviour
- **Feasibility** → practitioner calls, then a concierge run to find the real operational snags
- **Viability** → desk research on comparable costs, then pre-sale or pilot
- **Impact** → hardest to test early. Define the outcome you'd measure, check it's measurable at all, and use a pilot with a comparison group if the stakes justify it
- **Equity & harm** → recruit deliberately from the groups most likely to be left out; test with them first, not last

## Ethics

- Tell participants the truth, at the latest straight after the test.
- Never take money for something that doesn't exist. Pledges and deposits must be clearly refundable.
- Collect only the personal data the test needs, and say what happens to it.
- Pay people for their time when you're asking for more than a few minutes, especially people on low incomes.
- Don't run tests that could cause real harm if the thing tested were to fail — for example, withdrawing an existing service to see who notices.
