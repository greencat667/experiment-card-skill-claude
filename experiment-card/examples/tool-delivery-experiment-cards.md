# The idea

Our scenario work on community tool libraries found one no-regret move that holds up in all four futures: **reach the people other models miss, meaning renters, car-free households and people on low incomes.** Doorstep delivery is the first idea we want to test against it.

> **For** people renting flats without a car **who** occasionally need a bulky tool but have nowhere to store one and no easy way to carry one home, **a tool library that delivers and collects by cargo bike** **will** let them borrow instead of buying or going without, **unlike** today, where borrowing means a trip to a hub during volunteer opening hours.

**Decision owner:** Programme Lead
**Decision needed by:** end of January 2027, in time to include delivery (or not) in our bid to the council's climate fund
**Already committed:** nothing beyond staff time on this exercise

# Assumption map

Evidence is graded on the 0–5 ladder: 0 belief · 1 opinion · 2 analogy · 3 small commitment · 4 real commitment · 5 sustained behaviour. Importance asks what happens to the idea if the assumption is false: 5 means it dies, 3 means a major redesign, 1 means a detail changes.

**A belief we're deliberately not relying on:** the popular claim that a drill is used for only 12–13 minutes in its lifetime. We traced it to an uncited 2010 blog post ([Nesta](https://www.nesta.org.uk/blog/do-you-own-a-drill/)), and it has been challenged ([Nicholas Carr](https://www.roughtype.com/?p=6527)). It's a grade-0 belief, not evidence. It's also beside the point: the question is whether people *need* a tool occasionally, not how long they use it.

# Riskiest assumptions

Plotting importance against evidence:

| | **Little evidence (0–1)** | **Some evidence (2+)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical (4–5)** | **D3** choose delivery · D6 repeat use · F3 insurance · V1 costs · I1 replaces purchases · E2 fee excludes | **D1** real need · **D4** pay £6 · **E1** reaches lower incomes · D2 barriers · F2 courier · V2 council funds |
| **Less critical (1–3)** | F4 booking system · E3 rider safety | D5 time windows · F1 bulky items · V3 commercial rivals · I2 emissions |

We'll test three first:

1. **D1 — real need among lower-income renters without cars.** Everything else depends on this. The statistics tell us the group exists, not that it has the problem. Testing it with lower-income renters specifically also gives us early evidence on **E1**, the assumption with the most worrying evidence so far.
2. **D4 — willingness to pay about £6.** This is critical and only supported by analogy, and online shopping evidence suggests delivery fees are a common reason people give up.
3. **D3 — choosing delivery over the alternatives.** The idea dies without it, and we have no evidence at all. It can only be tested properly by offering the real thing.

**Deliberately deferred:**

- **F3 (insurance)** is critical but isn't something users can tell us. We'll ask our insurer this week, in parallel, as a desk check. If they can't cover it, Experiment 3 doesn't run.
- **V1 (costs)** depends on a courier quote, which we'll get alongside F3. We'll track real costs during Experiment 3 rather than test them separately.
- **I1 (replacing purchases)** is a leap-of-faith assumption. It's critical and hard to test cheaply, because it needs longer-term data on what people would otherwise have done. We'll ask a simple proxy question in Experiment 3 and plan a proper test for a pilot.

# Experiment cards

## Experiment 1 — Is the need real?

**Assumption:** We believe that renters in flats without a car regularly need occasional-use tools, and currently buy, go without or struggle.

**Lens:** Desirability (and Equity) · **Importance:** 5 · **Evidence now:** grade 2

| | |
|---|---|
| **To test it, we will** | Run 10 short problem interviews (20 minutes) with private or social renters in flats who don't have a car, recruited through our housing association partner's tenants' newsletter and a community pantry noticeboard. At least 6 of the 10 must be receiving a means-tested benefit or council tax reduction (self-declared). We'll ask about the last time they needed a tool or piece of equipment and what they did. We won't mention the delivery idea until the last five minutes. |
| **And measure** | Number of the 10 who describe a specific time in the last 12 months when they needed an occasional-use item and bought it, went without, or struggled to get it home |
| **We're right if** | 6 or more of 10 |
| **We'll stop or rethink if** | 2 or fewer of 10 |
| **In between means** | Inconclusive: run 6 more interviews, focusing on the group that reported the need |
| **Evidence this gives us** | Grade 1 for "whether" (it's what people say about the past); strong for "why" and for the words people use |
| **Also watch** | What they did instead (bought, borrowed from someone they know, went without); what stopped them borrowing; whether they'd heard of a tool library |
| **Cost** | £250 (10 × £25 vouchers) + 3 staff days |
| **Time** | Weeks 1–2 |
| **Owner** | Community Engagement Officer |

**If we're right:** run Experiment 3, and use participants' own words in the prototype and flyer copy.
**If we stop:** drop delivery as the lead idea. Look at whatever they *did* describe struggling with.
**If inconclusive:** extend as above before deciding.

**Known limits:** People recall past needs imperfectly, and ten people can't tell us how common the need is across the area. Asking about the *last specific time*, rather than "do you ever…", reduces but doesn't remove the problem of people telling us what they think we want to hear.

## Experiment 2 — Will they get past the fee?

**Assumption:** We believe that they'll pay a delivery fee of around £6 per loan on top of the loan fee.

**Lens:** Desirability / Viability · **Importance:** 5 · **Evidence now:** grade 2

| | |
|---|---|
| **To test it, we will** | Run 8 moderated sessions with a clickable prototype of the booking journey, showing the real loan fee and a £6 delivery fee exactly where a live service would show them. Participants are renters in flats without a car (recruited as in Experiment 1, but different people). Each gets one realistic task: arrange a carpet cleaner for Saturday. |
| **And measure** | Number of the 8 who choose delivery and complete the booking without help, after seeing the delivery fee |
| **We're right if** | 5 or more of 8 |
| **We'll stop or rethink if** | 2 or fewer of 8 |
| **In between means** | Inconclusive: test a lower fee, or a free-first-delivery version, with 5 more people |
| **Evidence this gives us** | Grade 3 for "can they, and do they get stuck at the fee"; grade 1 for "would they really pay" |
| **Also watch** | Where people hesitate (from the prototype's log); what they say at the fee screen; how many look for a cheaper option; whether they understand the delivery window and what happens if the tool breaks |
| **Cost** | £200 (8 × £25 vouchers) + 4 staff days, including building the prototype |
| **Time** | Weeks 1–2, in parallel with Experiment 1 |
| **Owner** | Digital Officer |

**If we're right:** keep £6 for Experiment 3.
**If we stop:** redesign the pricing before Experiment 3, for example a free first delivery, a subscription, or fee-free delivery for people on means-tested benefits, and re-test with 5 people.
**If inconclusive:** as above.

**Known limits:** No real money changes hands, and people are more willing to click "confirm" in a test than to pay in real life. This test can spot a clear problem with the fee; it can't prove people will pay. That's what Experiment 3 is for.

## Experiment 3 — Will they actually book it?

**Assumption:** We believe that, given the choice, they'll book a delivered tool rather than borrow from someone they know, rent commercially or go without.

**Lens:** Desirability · **Importance:** 5 · **Evidence now:** grade 0

| | |
|---|---|
| **To test it, we will** | Run a four-week concierge service in two neighbourhoods with a high share of rented flats. We deliver and collect real tools by hand, using a local courier co-op or a staff member with a cargo bike, for the price set after Experiment 2. We'll promote it with 600 flyers through doors and our housing association partner's newsletter, with a short booking page and a phone number. |
| **And measure** | Number of paid delivered bookings from households in the two target neighbourhoods over four weeks |
| **We're right if** | 20 or more bookings |
| **We'll stop or rethink if** | 6 or fewer bookings |
| **In between means** | Inconclusive: run two more weeks with a revised offer, based on what bookers and non-bookers told us |
| **Evidence this gives us** | Grade 4 (real commitment: paid bookings) |
| **Also watch** | Repeat bookings within the four weeks (D6); share of bookers on means-tested benefits (E1); fee-waiver requests (E2); items requested and their weight (F1); real cost per round trip, including staff time (V1); a one-question proxy at return: "If this hadn't existed, what would you have done?" (I1) |
| **Cost** | About £1,500: courier costs (quote pending), leaflet printing and 6 staff days. Offset by booking fees |
| **Time** | Weeks 3–7, only after insurance is confirmed (F3) |
| **Owner** | Hub Coordinator |

**If we're right:** include delivery in the council bid, with the booking, cost and reach data as evidence. The next riskiest assumption is V1: can it be paid for at scale?
**If we stop:** don't include delivery in the bid. Report what we learned about why people didn't book.
**If inconclusive:** extend as above, once.

**Known limits:** Four weeks may catch a seasonal peak or a lull. Early bookers may be enthusiasts rather than typical users. Staff delivering by hand hides the real cost of doing it at scale, so we'll record every hour.

**Why these thresholds:** we set 20 bookings from about 600 households reached (roughly 3%) because below that level we couldn't make a credible case to the council that delivery changes who we reach. We have no benchmark for delivered tool loans, so this is **provisional**. We'll record why, and revisit it for the next card whatever the result.

# Pre-mortem

It's the end of week 7 and the results told us nothing useful. Why?

1. **We only reached enthusiasts.** The flyers were read by people already sympathetic to sharing. *Fix:* recruitment for Experiments 1 and 2 goes through the housing association and community pantry, with the benefit-receipt quota. In Experiment 3 we'll track where bookers heard about us.
2. **Something else changed at the same time.** A cold snap or a bank holiday changed demand. *Fix:* avoid bank-holiday weeks; note the weather and any local events in the log.
3. **The offer was too rough to be credible,** so people didn't trust a hand-run service with a phone number. *Fix:* use a short, clear booking page and state conditions such as damage and cancellations up front.
4. **We moved the goalposts.** 12 bookings felt like success, so we called it one. *Fix:* the thresholds are written here, now, and the learning card must quote them.
5. **Insurance wasn't sorted in time,** so Experiment 3 started late or ran without cover. *Fix:* it doesn't start without written confirmation.
